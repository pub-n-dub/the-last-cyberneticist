# Episode 3

## Title

`Ashby, Grey Walter, and Norbert Wiener`

## Opening

Before cybernetics became a historical word, it was a live attempt to understand how a system holds itself together, senses a changing world, and corrects its own behavior. If we skip that layer, a lot of what comes later sounds more mysterious than it really is.

## Script

You're listening to `The Last Cyberneticist`.

This is Episode 3, and this one is here because I realized there was a gap.

In the last episode, I raised the question of what is possible with cybernetics. But before I move too quickly into later papers, later robots, and later arguments about machine intelligence, it makes sense to stop and recover three figures who help define the ground more clearly.

Those figures are Norbert Wiener, Grey Walter, and W. Ross Ashby.

Not because they are the only names that matter, and not because I want to turn the show into a seminar, but because each of them helps answer a different part of the same question.

Wiener helps name the field.

Grey Walter helps show what behavior can look like in a machine when the mechanism is small but the interaction is rich.

Ashby helps explain why adaptation, regulation, and stability are not side issues, but the center of the problem.

That is a better bridge for this series than jumping straight from present-day AI talk into a much later architectural discussion.

So start with Wiener.

Norbert Wiener is the figure most people know, if they know any of them at all, because cybernetics in the public sense is tied to his name. Communication, control, feedback, signals, action, correction. That broad picture is still useful because it stops us from thinking about intelligence as if it were only a matter of abstract reasoning.

For Wiener, the question was never just whether a system computes. The question is whether it can receive information, compare conditions, respond to error, and alter conduct in time.

That matters because once you frame the problem that way, intelligence does not begin at the level of a chatbot or a theorem prover. It begins much lower down, where a system has to survive contact with the world.

Then you move to Grey Walter.

Grey Walter is important because he built machines that made cybernetic ideas visible. His tortoises were not impressive in the modern corporate sense. They were small, limited, and mechanically humble. But that is exactly why they still matter.

They showed that behavior can look surprisingly lifelike when a machine is organized around a few clear loops of sensing and action. Light seeking, obstacle response, reorientation, recharge behavior. Not a fantasy mind. Not a marketing promise. A real machine exhibiting real conduct because its organization let the environment participate in the behavior.

That is one of the deepest lessons in cybernetics.

You do not always get the most insight from the largest machine or the most expensive machine. Sometimes you get it from the machine whose limits are easiest to see.

And that is one reason Grey Walter belongs in this series. Because this show is not really about being impressed by scale. It is about understanding what a machine is doing when it senses, turns, stalls, recovers, or fails.

Then there is Ashby.

Ashby is the one who makes the problem harder in the best possible way.

Because once feedback is established, and once simple behavior is visible, the next question is not just how a system responds. The next question is how a system remains viable under changing conditions.

How much variety can it meet?

How does it regulate itself?

What has to be preserved for the system to keep functioning at all?

And under what conditions can it adapt rather than merely repeat?

Ashby gives cybernetics some of its sharpest vocabulary for those questions. He forces us to think about the relation between the complexity of the world and the complexity of the regulator. He forces us to ask whether a machine can actually absorb disturbance, not just perform in a demonstration.

That is essential for where this podcast is going.

Because the difference between a machine that merely runs and a machine that remains coherent through trouble is not small. That difference is where repair, embodiment, and adaptation all begin to touch.

So if you put Wiener, Grey Walter, and Ashby together, you get a very strong bridge.

Wiener names the field of communication and control.

Grey Walter shows that embodied behavior can emerge from a disciplined sensorimotor organization.

Ashby asks what kind of regulator can endure disturbance and preserve viability.

And once you have that triangle in mind, later work starts to sound less isolated.

That is where Berkeley and Heiserman will come in next.

Because Berkeley can be heard more clearly as part of this broader effort to describe sensing, control, state, storage, and action in organized machines. And Heiserman can be heard more clearly as part of the adaptive deepening of that same problem, not as an eccentric side road detached from the main history.

This also helps keep the series honest.

If I go too quickly from modern AI dissatisfaction into later theoretical papers, it can sound like I am merely substituting one abstraction for another. But Wiener, Walter, and Ashby keep the conversation closer to the actual ground of cybernetics: systems, environments, signals, limits, correction, persistence.

They make it easier to ask the right question.

Not: how do we make the biggest claim for intelligence?

But: what does a real system have to do to remain organized in the world?

That is a much better question for the bench.

And it is a much better question for a robot.

Because once a machine has wheels, sensors, drift, voltage, steering faults, memory limits, and real timing constraints, cybernetics stops being a slogan. It becomes a test.

So this is why I wanted to insert this episode here.

Before Berkeley and Heiserman.

Before the repair bench becomes the center of the narrative.

Before the machine itself starts forcing the argument.

We need this older triangle in view.

Wiener for communication and control.

Grey Walter for visible embodied behavior.

Ashby for adaptation, regulation, and viability.

If those three are in place, then the rest of the series can proceed with better footing.

In the next episode, I'll use Berkeley and Heiserman to recover a more explicit architectural vocabulary for the same terrain. And after that, the bench gets its turn to answer back.

## Closing

Before we ask what a machine can become, we may need to remember how cybernetics first learned to describe what a machine is doing at all.
