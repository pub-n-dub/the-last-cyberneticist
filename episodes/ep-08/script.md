# Episode 8: It is the Difference that Matters

## Script

Welcome to Episode 8 of *The Last Cyberneticist*.

This episode is called *It is the Difference that Matters*.

In the previous episode, I stayed with a very small machine and asked what it means for a machine to remember. An address was selected. A four-bit value was written. Later, that address was selected again and the value could be read back. There is nothing grand about that operation, and that is why it is useful. A state was made, retained for a time, and found again.

But memory alone is not guidance. A remembered condition may be present, but until it is placed in relation to something, it does not tell a system what is at stake. The word in a memory chip is just a word. A temperature in a body is just a temperature. A sound in the air is just a variation in pressure. What changes the situation is not mere detection, and not mere storage. It is the moment when a difference becomes consequential.

We use the word comparison so casually that it has acquired a rather bad reputation. We think of comparative judgment as a social injury: better and worse, richer and poorer, ahead and behind, successful and unsuccessful. Those comparisons can be corrosive because they ask us to measure a life against an external ranking. But comparison in its older and more fundamental sense is not a competition. It is a way of finding a relation.

There is no comparison without a distinction, but a distinction alone is not yet a comparison. Two things can differ without that difference entering anyone's life. Comparison begins when a difference is held in a form that can guide an answer: this rather than that; more rather than less; now rather than then; inside a limit or outside it. It gives a system an orientation. Not a final truth, not necessarily a conscious decision, but a way for what is present to be measured against what is possible, expected, required, or remembered.

Too hot or too cold. Nearer or farther away. Safe footing or a loss of balance. Enough water or not enough. The smell that belongs to food, the sound that belongs to danger, the pressure that tells a hand it has made contact with the world. Before there is a theory of the world, there is a body for which some differences matter and many do not.

That is where I want to begin: not with an electronic component, and not with the fashionable language of artificial intelligence, but with the plain fact that a living thing must remain in a relation with conditions around and within it. It cannot respond to every difference in the universe. There are too many. The world is full of changes in light, pressure, temperature, chemical composition, motion, sound, and force. A living system survives by being organized so that some of those differences can enter its own continuing activity.

This is not the same as saying that every organism is a little computer with a comparator hidden inside it. That is one of the temptations of cybernetics when it is used badly: a diagram becomes a universal explanation, and the living world is flattened until it resembles the diagram. The diagram can be useful, but it is not the thing. An organism is not a thermostat with poetry added. It is a body with a history, limits, needs, senses, capacities for action, and a situation that changes as it acts.

Still, the cybernetic language gives us a way to ask a precise question. What must be true of a system if a difference is to have consequences for what it does next?

Walter Cannon used the word homeostasis to describe the maintenance of conditions that make bodily life possible. The word is often simplified into a picture of a body trying to hold everything still. But that cannot be right. Life does not hold still. It circulates, repairs, digests, sleeps, wakes, grows, heals, ages, and sometimes fails. The important thought is not immobility. It is that a body can remain viable through change.

The internal conditions of an organism do not simply receive whatever the outside world delivers. They are actively maintained, within limits, through many interconnected processes. Blood chemistry, temperature, water balance, oxygen, posture, and innumerable other conditions are not matters of indifference. They are not all kept at one perfect number by one master controller. They are held, negotiated, and repaired through distributed processes that have been shaped by the life of the organism.

That distinction matters for this series. When we say that a system regulates itself, we should not imagine a little person inside it consulting a dial. Regulation does not require a homunculus. It requires an organization in which changes can be sensed, can affect other processes, and can lead to responses that tend to keep the system within conditions in which it can continue.

W. Ross Ashby gave this a particularly severe and useful form. He wrote about what he called essential variables: the conditions that must remain within certain bounds if the organism is to survive. This lets us speak without mystification. Survival is not an inner feeling. It can be described as the continued avoidance of certain destructive limits. A system that can keep its essential variables from leaving their viable ranges has achieved something real. A system that cannot, eventually ceases to be the system it was.

There is a humility in that definition that I like. It does not begin by asking whether a creature is clever. It asks whether it can remain viable. It does not begin with a grand theory of mind. It begins with the more difficult fact that a body has limits, and that it has to find ways of not exceeding them.

Ashby is especially important here because he does not ask us to treat adaptation as magic. An adaptive system is not a system that has been given the label adaptive. It is one whose organization allows it to find, preserve, or recover forms of behaviour that keep its essential conditions in bounds. The word adaptation names a real achievement, but it also names a problem in design. How does a system encounter disturbance, register that something has changed, and alter what it does in a way that improves its chance of continuing?

That question is already comparative. There is a present condition. There is a range of conditions in which continuation remains possible. There are changes that move toward or away from that range. There are actions that have effects. The system need not contain a sentence saying, “I am now comparing my present state with my desired state.” It may have no sentence at all. But it must be organized so that relevant differences can enter its conduct.

This is where we have to become careful with the word relevant. Relevant to whom? Relevant to what?

Jakob von Uexküll offered one of the most beautiful answers. He argued that each organism lives in a particular surrounding world—an *Umwelt*—formed by what it can sense and what it can do. There is not simply one finished environment, presented in the same way to every creature. The tick, the dog, the bird, the tree, and the person inhabit overlapping physical circumstances, but not the same world of significance.

For a tick, a very small number of differences can organize an entire course of life. The scent of butyric acid can signal the presence of a mammal. Warmth and the character of skin can direct the next action. It would be foolish to describe the tick as a miniature philosopher making a detached comparison. But it would be equally foolish to say that it inhabits no meaningful world. Its world is narrow, exact, and bound to the actions its body can perform.

The point is not that human beings are merely more complicated ticks. The point is that significance is not sprinkled evenly over reality. A difference becomes a sign, a warning, an opportunity, or a demand within a relation between a particular organization and the world it can meet. Perception and action belong together. A perceived difference is not finished at the sense organ; it is part of a possible cycle of conduct.

That is already a correction to much current talk about intelligence. We are often asked to marvel at a system that has consumed an enormous body of text, images, or records. But accumulation is not relation. A system may contain a great deal of stored material and still have no stake in any of it. It may classify a difference without needing to preserve itself through that classification. It may produce a convincing sentence about pain without having anything that can be hurt. None of this makes the system useless. It simply means that we should not smuggle the conditions of living into a machine by calling its output intelligent.

Gregory Bateson gave cybernetics a phrase that has survived because it catches something important: information is a “difference that makes a difference.” It is a good phrase, but it is often used as decoration. The difficult part is the second difference. A change in the world is not automatically information. It becomes information only where there is an organized path capable of being affected by it and transforming what happens next.

Think of a door closing in another building. It creates vibrations. Those vibrations are real whether or not anyone hears them. But if they reach a body with ears, attention, memory, expectation, and the capacity to act, they may become a signal: someone has arrived, something has gone wrong, there is a threat, there is a friend, there is nothing to do. The physical change is not identical with its significance. Significance arises in an organized relation.

That relation is not merely inside a head. The energy that crosses the room is not supplied by a thought. The muscles that turn the head, the body that moves toward a door, the floor that supports the body, and the next sound that confirms or corrects the first impression are all part of the event. Bateson’s strength is that he refuses to let us put mind in one sealed container. Difference travels through a circuit of organism and surroundings, and it is transformed along the way.

This is why comparative analysis is not an academic luxury. It is one of the conditions of contact. To compare is not only to put two abstract quantities side by side. It is to allow one condition to alter the significance of another. A current position is judged in relation to a possible position. A remembered route is revised in relation to an obstacle. A bodily state becomes alarming in relation to what the organism can bear. An action is retained, abandoned, or varied in relation to what follows from it.

The word judgment is perhaps too lofty for many of these processes. A plant turning toward light is not conducting a seminar. A person withdrawing a hand from a flame is not first forming a proposition. A bird correcting its flight in wind is not necessarily calculating its trajectory in the way an engineer would. Yet all of these cases involve an organization in which differences can enter action. The organism does not merely undergo the world. It is coupled to it.

That coupling is the territory cybernetics tried to recover. Norbert Wiener gave us feedback as a way to describe effects returning into the conditions of further action. Ashby gave us the problem of stability and adaptation. Grey Walter built small machines whose conduct became interesting because sensors, motors, obstacles, light, and internal organization were allowed to meet in loops. Their importance was never that they proved a machine was alive. Their importance was that they made the shape of a loop visible.

There is a further distinction we need if we are going to make honest machines. Detecting a difference is not the same as comparing it. Comparing is not the same as evaluating it. Evaluating is not the same as selecting an action. Selecting an action is not the same as learning from its result.

Those stages are often collapsed in contemporary language. A sensor becomes perception. A stored record becomes memory. A statistical adjustment becomes learning. A response becomes agency. The language gets ahead of the architecture, and soon nobody can inspect what the machine actually does. The small board on the bench is valuable because it refuses this collapse. It can show us one stage at a time.

Episode 7 dealt with retention. A selected address in the `MM2114A` static RAM can retain a four-bit word while power and the control conditions remain correct. That is memory in a restricted but entirely real sense. It gives us a state that can be returned to and verified. But the board does not, by that fact alone, know whether the stored word is good, bad, useful, dangerous, close, or far away. Those are relations, not properties contained in a number by itself.

To produce even a minimal relation, we need two distinct terms. We need a remembered word. And we need another word against which it can be placed.

That is where the Four-Bit Wonder receives its small extension.

The extension is a `74LS85`, a four-bit magnitude comparator. It accepts two four-bit binary words and reports one of three relations: the first is lower, the two are equal, or the first is higher. In the board’s demonstration, one input comes from the word read from the SRAM. The other comes from the four data switches, where a person selects a target. Green can mean low, or increase. Red can mean match. Yellow can mean high, or decrease.

Notice how little, and how much, this says. The result does not explain why the words differ. It does not tell us how far apart they are in a form that can be acted on. It offers no plan. Yet it supplies direction: below, at, or above a selected condition. For a later controller, that direction could become a reason to hold, increase, or decrease. On this board it remains an exposed relation, available for a person to inspect.

This is not life in silicon. It is not adaptation. It is not an artificial creature. The comparator does not care which result appears, and the lamps do not change the stored word. The target is supplied by a person. The candidate is supplied by the memory. No part of the board yet selects an action, retains a target independently, steps a candidate toward that target, writes the revision, or checks what happened afterward.

But the comparator does make one indispensable condition visible. It lets a stored state stand in relation to a demand. It gives the machine a vocabulary of difference: lower, equal, higher.

Even here, the discipline of the board matters. The two comparator inputs must remain genuinely separate. If the switch-selected target and the SRAM read value were carelessly tied to the same bus, the comparison would only tell us that a signal matches itself. And the result is meaningful only while the SRAM is selected and actively being read. During a write, or when the memory output is in a high-impedance state, a lamp may still glow, but it is no longer evidence of a valid remembered word.

That is a small technical warning with a larger philosophical use. A visible result is not automatically a meaningful result. We have to know what relation produced it, what the system was doing at the time, and whether the conditions for interpretation were actually present. The same rule should govern every large claim we make about machines.

The Four-Bit Wonder does not answer the question of life. It gives us a way to hold one part of that question in our hands. A remembered state becomes more than a retained mark when it can be placed against another condition. A difference becomes more than a difference when the organization of a system lets it matter.

Next time, we will go inside that small technical arrangement. We will ask what is physically required to keep target, memory, bus, timing, and control states distinct; and what further architecture would be needed before a displayed difference could enter a real cycle of revision.

Thank you for listening.
