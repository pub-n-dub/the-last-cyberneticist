# Berkeley As Center of Gravity, Not Final Horizon

Berkeley should remain the center of gravity for *The Last Cyberneticist*, but not the sole horizon.

He gives the project its sharpest and most distinctive claim: machine intelligence understood not as disembodied calculation alone, but as organized, embodied, stateful conduct. If we lose Berkeley as the anchor, the project risks flattening into a general survey of cybernetics.

At the same time, the evolutionary pivot in the project now has a clearer reason. Wiener’s `Time and Organization` reframes the issue at a higher level of abstraction. It treats control and communication not as features of one special machine, but as principles that can be embodied in organisms, machines, and even social organization. It also pushes the problem into time: clocks, coordination, sequence, synchronization, storage, and the organization of activity across intervals rather than at a single instant. That matters because it reveals that Berkeley is strongest not merely as an isolated author of symbolic machine thought, but as one especially clear entry point into a larger problem of organized intelligence in time.

That is why the horizon ahead is not just a widened cast of historical characters. It is the reconstruction of a broader lineage of living machine intelligence:

- Shannon as the bridge from symbolic logic to hardware realization
- Wiener and Ashby as the wider frame of control, communication, regulation, and organization
- Walter as the concrete demonstration of embodied, world-facing behavior
- Heiserman as the later deepening of memory, generalization, and adaptive revision
- Turing as the nearby but different developmental route

So the strategic direction is not `Berkeley only`, and not `beyond Berkeley` in the sense of leaving him behind. It is `Berkeley first, then outward under discipline`.

Wiener helps explain why that pivot is necessary. Once intelligence is understood as organized conduct unfolding through time, the project can no longer remain only a recovery of Berkeley’s vocabulary. It has to ask how persistence, recoverability, coordination, timing, memory, and adaptation are realized across different kinds of systems. Berkeley remains the protagonist, but the deeper payoff is to show that he opens onto an underdescribed tradition of organized embodied intelligence that still exposes gaps in contemporary AI and robotics.

In short: keep Berkeley at the center, but let Wiener’s temporal and organizational horizon explain the expansion. The project is strongest when Berkeley serves as the entry point to a still unfinished research program rather than as an isolated historical curiosity.
