# Manifesto: Berkeley and the Next Horizon

Berkeley is not a footnote to symbolic AI. He is a central figure in an unfinished architecture of embodied machine intelligence.

He should remain the center of gravity for *The Last Cyberneticist* because he gives the project its strongest claim: intelligence is not only calculation, but organized conduct realized in machinery. In Berkeley, symbolic logic is not merely formal analysis. It becomes design, control, state, action, and persistence.

But Berkeley should not be treated as a closed destination. He is the threshold.

The reason for the project’s evolutionary pivot is now clearer in light of Wiener’s `Time and Organization`. Wiener raises the problem from a single historical figure to a general architecture. Control and communication are treated as principles that can be embodied in machines, organisms, and social organization alike. More importantly, organization is treated as temporal: systems must coordinate activity, sequence operations, store signals, synchronize parts, and remain intelligible across time. Once that horizon is visible, the project can no longer remain a narrow Berkeley recovery. It has to become an inquiry into organized intelligence as a time-structured phenomenon.

Beyond Berkeley, a wider field comes into view:

- Shannon makes logical form technically realizable in hardware.
- Wiener and Ashby define the broader horizon of control, communication, regulation, and organization.
- Walter demonstrates that embodied behavior can arise through simple but concrete circuitry.
- Heiserman deepens the architecture through memory, generalization, and adaptive revision.
- Turing preserves a neighboring path in which intelligence develops through training and organization rather than explicit control design alone.

This is the real horizon ahead: not a rotation from one historical figure to another, but the recovery of a neglected research program. Living machine intelligence is the problem. Its marks are state, embodiment, persistence, recoverability, adaptation, timing, and organized behavior across time.

That claim is larger than Berkeley, but Berkeley is where it becomes visible with unusual clarity.

So the direction is simple: keep Berkeley at the center, let Wiener explain the expansion, move outward through disciplined comparison, and use that reconstruction to challenge thinner contemporary accounts of AI that still struggle to describe intelligence as durable embodied organization.
